How to Travel Europe with a Dog Successfully

NeonVagabond
23.07.2026 • Read time: 5 min

Imagine strolling down the cobblestone streets of Paris, sipping an espresso at an outdoor café in Rome, or hiking the dramatic green ridges of the Scottish Highlands—all with your four-legged best friend trotting right beside you. Europe is arguably one of the most dog-friendly continents on the planet. Unlike many parts of the world where dogs are routinely banned from public spaces, European culture largely embraces canine companions. From riding the underground metro in Berlin to dining inside upscale restaurants in France, traveling Europe with a dog is not just a dream; it is an entirely achievable reality.

However, turning this dream into reality requires meticulous planning, an understanding of international pet travel laws, and a bit of street smarts. Navigating borders, health certificates, and varying public transit rules can feel overwhelming at first glance. This comprehensive guide will walk you through everything you need to know to ensure a seamless, tail-wagging adventure across Europe.

1. The Legalities: Preparing Your Dog for European Entry

Before you book that flight or ferry, you must satisfy the strict entry requirements set by the European Union (EU) for pet travel. Failing to meet even one of these requirements can result in your pet being placed in mandatory quarantine at your own expense—or worse, sent back home.

The EU Pet Passport vs. Animal Health Certificate

If you are traveling from outside the EU (such as the US, Canada, or Australia), your dog cannot get an EU Pet Passport initially. Instead, you will need a non-commercial EU Animal Health Certificate issued by a certified veterinarian. This document is country-specific, time-sensitive (usually must be issued within 10 days of entering the EU), and requires official endorsement by your home country's department of agriculture.

Mandatory Microchip and Rabies Vaccination

Before any paperwork can be filed, your dog must be fitted with an ISO-compliant 15-digit microchip. Crucially, the microchip must be implanted before or on the exact same day as your dog's rabies vaccination. If your dog gets vaccinated for rabies before being microchipped, that vaccination does not count in the eyes of EU officials, and you will have to start over.

Tapeworm Treatment for Specific Countries

If your itinerary includes entering Ireland, Finland, Malta, Norway, or Northern Ireland, your dog must receive a specific tapeworm treatment (praziquantel-based) administered by a veterinarian between 24 and 120 hours before entering these specific territories. This must be officially recorded in your paperwork.

2. Getting Around: Navigating European Transit with a Canine

One of the greatest joys of exploring the continent is its interconnected transit network. Thankfully, Europeans love their pets, and most public transit systems are remarkably accommodating. Knowing how to use public transport in Europe like a pro will save you hours of stress and ensure a smooth ride for both you and your pet.

Here is a breakdown of how different transit modes handle dogs:

Transit Type General Policy Tips for Success
Trains (High-Speed & Regional) Most national rail networks (SNCF, Deutsche Bahn, Trenitalia) allow dogs. Small dogs in carriers usually travel free; larger dogs need a ticket (usually half-fare) and a leash/muzzle. Check specific country rules. In some places (like Spain), only dogs under 10 kg are allowed on high-speed trains, while others welcome all sizes.
Buses and Trams Widely allowed in major cities, often requiring a muzzle or a secure carrier box. Avoid rush hour. Pushing through a packed commuter tram with a large dog is stressful for everyone involved.
Domestic Flights Airlines like Lufthansa, Air France, and KLM allow small dogs in the cabin if they fit in an approved soft carrier under the seat. Book your pet's spot well in advance; cabins have strict caps on the number of animals allowed per flight.
Ferries Many cross-border ferries offer pet-friendly cabins or designated kennel spaces. Book pet-friendly cabins months in advance, as they sell out faster than standard rooms.

When you are ready to venture across borders, understanding how to travel between European countries easily will help you map out whether a high-speed train or a scenic international bus ride is the best fit for your traveling pack.

3. Finding Accommodation: Where to Stay

Finding dog-friendly lodging in Europe is easier than ever, thanks to platforms like Booking.com, Airbnb, and specialized sites like BringFido. However, "dog-friendly" means different things in different countries.

In countries like Germany, Austria, and France, dogs are frequently allowed in hotel rooms, and some high-end hotels even roll out the red carpet with dog beds, bowls, and welcome treats. In contrast, Southern and Eastern Europe may have more restricted options, though the landscape is rapidly changing.

  • Always notify the hotel in advance: Even if a listing says "pets allowed," let the front desk know the breed and size of your dog so they can assign you a ground-floor room or a room close to an exit.
  • Inquire about fees: Some hotels charge a flat cleaning fee, while others charge per night. Clarify this beforehand to avoid surprises at checkout.
  • Never leave your dog unattended: European hotels generally have strict policies against leaving barking or roaming dogs alone in a hotel room unless you are physically present.

4. Dining Out and Social Etiquette

One of the most remarkable aspects of European dog culture is dining out. In countries like Italy, France, Spain, and Germany, it is completely normal to bring your dog into restaurants, pubs, and sidewalk cafés. In fact, many waiters will bring your dog a bowl of fresh water before they even take your drink order!

To maintain this warm hospitality, you must practice good canine etiquette:

  • Keep your dog under control: Your dog should lie quietly under the table or beside your chair, well out of the path of busy servers carrying hot plates.
  • Mind the local laws: While street cafés are almost universally dog-friendly, indoor establishments that prepare food (like traditional bakeries or butcher shops) may restrict dogs for health and safety regulations. Look for signs on the door showing a dog silhouette.
  • Socialize before you go: Ensure your dog is comfortable in loud, crowded environments filled with the smells of delicious food and the commotion of chatting patrons.

5. Packing Checklist for Your Euro-Dog

Packing for a canine traveler requires foresight. While you can buy pet supplies anywhere in Europe, having your dog's favorites on hand prevents upset stomachs and adjustment stress.

  • Travel Documents: Animal Health Certificate, rabies vaccination records, and pet insurance papers (ideally with international coverage).
  • Comfortable Gear: A sturdy harness, a standard leash (retractable leashes can be hazardous on crowded European streets), and a comfortable, airline-approved carrier if using public transit.
  • Muzzle: Even if your dog is an absolute angel, carry a lightweight, comfortable basket muzzle. Many European transit networks and cable cars legally require dogs to wear one, regardless of temperament.
  • First Aid Kit: Tick removal tools, paw balm for hot pavement or salted winter roads, bandages, and any prescription medications your dog needs.
  • Collapsible Bowls and Food: Pack enough of their regular food for the first week to prevent digestive issues while transitioning to local brands.

6. Seasonal Considerations

Europe’s diverse climate means your travel experience will change dramatically depending on the season. If you are planning a winter getaway to experience the magical must-visit Christmas markets in Europe for your next trip, keep in mind that massive crowds, freezing temperatures, and loud holiday music can be overwhelming for some dogs. Consider leaving your pup in a cozy hotel room or pet-sitter's care during peak market hours.

Conversely, summer travel in Southern Europe (like Greece, Spain, and Italy) brings intense heat waves. Pavement can reach scorching temperatures that burn paw pads, and heatstroke is a real danger. Plan your walks for early mornings and late evenings, and always carry extra water.

Final Thoughts

Traveling through Europe with your dog is an enriching, unforgettable bonding experience. By respecting local laws, understanding public transit, and planning your itinerary with your dog's comfort in mind, you will unlock a side of Europe that few tourists get to see. Pack your bags, grab the leash, and get ready for the ultimate European adventure with your best friend!
